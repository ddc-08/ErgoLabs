Experiment A · Decisions under uncertainty

Would you play a game with positive expected returns?

An appealing average can hide a very different individual experience.

What does “expected return” mean?

Expected value is an average across possible outcomes, weighted by their probabilities. A 50% chance of receiving $10 and a 50% chance of receiving $0 has an expected value of $5. It is not a guaranteed payment.

A positive expected return means that this probability-weighted average gain is above zero.

Game rules

Each player starts with $100 and plays 100 rounds. In every round, an independent fair coin toss determines the change in their current wealth.

Each change applies to current wealth. All wealth is exposed each round. No deposits, withdrawals, borrowing or pooling. Outcomes are independent across players and rounds.

04 / Understand the mechanism

Wealth multiplies. Logarithms add.

Expected wealth uses an arithmetic average.

E[X] = ½(1.5) + ½(0.6) = 1.05
E[Wₜ] = $100 × 1.05ᵗ
About this calculation

The expected multiplier is 1.05. Independence makes expected wealth multiply by this factor each round.

Expected wealth rises by 5% per round. This does not say what most individuals receive.

An individual's wealth is a product.

Wₜ / W₀ = X₁ × X₂ × … × Xₜ
One win and one loss: 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9
About this calculation

One win and one loss give 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9: a 10% loss over two rounds, in either order.

Logs turn the product into a sum.

ln(Wₜ / W₀) = ln X₁ + … + ln Xₜ
gₜ = ln(Wₜ / W₀) / t
About this calculation

Here gₜ is realised average log growth per round. Natural logarithms are used.

Average the log increments.

gₜ → g = E[ln X] almost surely
g = ½ ln(1.5) + ½ ln(0.6)
g ≈ −0.052680 per round
About this calculation

For independent, identically distributed log multipliers, the law of large numbers makes their time average converge to their expectation.

We have not assumed a preference for logarithmic utility. The logarithm arises from compounding under the chosen growth objective.

Convert back to a compounded percentage.

exp(g) = √0.9 ≈ 0.948683
exp(g) − 1 ≈ −5.13% per round
About this calculation

This is an equivalent constant compounded rate—not the actual return in any round. Actual returns remain +50% or −40%.

Why the two growth measures differ

ln(E[X]) = ln(1.05) ≈ +0.048790
E[ln X] ≈ −0.052680
50 wins and 50 losses: $100 × 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ $0.52
Why these averages differ

The log of an average is not the average of the logs. The logarithm is strictly concave, so Jensen's inequality gives E[ln X] < ln(E[X]) for this nonconstant multiplier.

Exactly 50 wins and 50 losses leave $100 × 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ $0.52. Not every run ends there. The curve $100 × exp(gt) is a growth benchmark, not a guaranteed path or an exact median at every horizon.

How this connects to ergodicity

Return to the opening definition: an average over time can match an average across possibilities. Here, each round's log multiplier has the same distribution and is independent of the others. Its average along a long history converges to its expectation.

Wealth itself has a changing distribution over time: it is nonstationary. The stationary ergodic theorem therefore does not apply directly to wealth levels. A gap between the sample mean and median alone does not establish non-ergodicity.

“Time-average growth” means the long-run rate ln(Wₜ/W₀)/t—not the arithmetic time average of wealth levels.

For a fixed horizon, increasing the number of independent players makes the sample mean converge to expected wealth. Increasing the number of rounds for one player is a different limit. This does not invalidate expected value.

Your conclusion

Back to the question: would you play?

A positive expected return alone does not settle the decision. Rare, very large outcomes pull up expected wealth. Yet a player has about an 86% chance of finishing below $100 after 100 rounds, and the long-run compounded growth rate is negative.

This links back to ergodicity through the quantity we average. The average log change along one history approaches its expectation across possible outcomes. Wealth itself has a changing distribution, so the stationary ergodic theorem does not apply directly to wealth levels.

Expected wealth and individual compounded growth answer different questions. Would you keep your original answer? Consider your objective, time horizon and how much wealth is exposed.

What if you cannot keep playing?

A different model, inspired by Taleb’s discussion of interrupted participation. Experiment B will explore irreversible ruin in more detail.

The original game never reaches exactly zero in a finite number of rounds. Negative growth is not the same as hitting an absorbing barrier.

Now add a rule

Stop permanently after wealth falls below $10. Remaining money is held as cash with zero return. This is an illustrative exit threshold—not zero-wealth bankruptcy.

Logarithmic axis. Stopping is checked after each outcome, so wealth may fall below $10 before exit. Exited players remain represented.

Without stopping, reordering the same multipliers leaves terminal wealth unchanged. With an exit rule, order affects participation in later rounds.

This extension applies Taleb's emphasis on repeated exposure and interrupted participation. Baseline formulas no longer describe the stopped strategy.

Survival, exposure and limits of the analogy

Taleb distinguishes an individual's survival from that of a group or larger system. This experiment concerns an individual only.

Davide's essay connects exposure and survival to insurance and sustainable performance. Those are further questions, not results established by this coin toss. Insurance can improve a specified growth objective at some premiums, not all. Reducing exposure does not automatically make wealth formally ergodic.
