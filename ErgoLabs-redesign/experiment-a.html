The model’s assumptions

All current wealth is exposed. Tosses are fair and independent across players and rounds. There is no income, spending, borrowing, pooling or early stopping. Balances can include fractions of a cent.

Reproduce or change the sample

The seed determines every toss. Reusing the same seed reproduces the original website’s 1,000 × 100 simulation. Changing it resets the selected player to 1.

The balance changes. So does the amount at risk.

A 50% gain and a 40% loss do not cancel out: 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9. Repeat that pair 50 times and $100 becomes about $0.52.

X is the multiplier for one round: 1.5 for heads or 0.6 for tails. W₀ is starting wealth; Wₜ is wealth after t rounds. E[ ] means expected value, ln is the natural logarithm, and exp is its inverse.

Calculate expected wealth

E[X] = ½(1.5) + ½(0.6) = 1.05
E[Wₜ] = $100 × 1.05ᵗ
About this calculation

Each round has an expected multiplier of 1.05. Because tosses are independent, expected wealth after t rounds is $100 × 1.05ᵗ.

Expected wealth rises by 5% per round. This does not say what most individuals receive.

Calculate one player’s wealth over successive rounds

Wₜ / W₀ = X₁ × X₂ × … × Xₜ
One win and one loss: 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9
About this calculation

One win and one loss give 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9: a 10% loss over two rounds, in either order.

Logs turn the product into a sum

ln(Wₜ / W₀) = ln X₁ + … + ln Xₜ
gₜ = ln(Wₜ / W₀) / t

The logarithm turns multiplication into addition. Divide the total log change by the number of rounds, t, to find gₜ, the average log growth per round. This is not an average of the player’s dollar balances.

Find the long-run average log growth

gₜ → g = E[ln X] almost surely
g = ½ ln(1.5) + ½ ln(0.6)
g ≈ −0.052680 per round
About this calculation

With independent tosses and the same probabilities each round, the average log multiplier approaches its expected value as play continues. This is an application of the law of large numbers.

Logarithms let us calculate growth when wealth changes through multiplication. Their use here does not assume anything about the player’s preferences.

Express the growth rate as a percentage

exp(g) = √0.9 ≈ 0.948683
exp(g) − 1 ≈ −5.13% per round
About this calculation

A constant loss of about 5.13% per round would give the same long-run compound growth rate. The game itself still gives +50% or −40% each round.

Why the two growth measures differ

ln(E[X]) = ln(1.05) ≈ +0.048790
E[ln X] ≈ −0.052680
50 wins and 50 losses: $100 × 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ $0.52
Why these averages differ

The order of calculation matters: ln(E[X]) takes the logarithm after averaging, while E[ln X] averages the logarithms. Here, E[ln X] < ln(E[X]). This follows from the downward curvature of the logarithm, expressed by Jensen’s inequality.

50 heads and 50 tails leave about $0.52. The curve $100 × exp(gt) follows the constant growth rate g. It does not predict an individual player’s wealth or give the exact median at every round.

How this connects to ergodicity

For each round, take the logarithm of the wealth multiplier. Its average over many rounds approaches its expected value, −0.052680. This is where averaging over time agrees with averaging across possible outcomes.

Wealth itself is not stationary: its possible values and their probabilities change over time. The result above applies to log changes, not directly to wealth levels.

Adding more independent players helps estimate expected wealth at round 100. Letting one player continue for more rounds measures growth over time. These are different calculations.

The long-run compound rate is −5.13% per round. That is a limit for repeated play, not the return of every player after 100 tosses.

What this says about ergodicity

Ergodicity concerns whether averages over time and across possible outcomes agree for the same quantity. Here, the average log change along a history approaches its expected value. Average wealth across players does not describe one person’s compound growth.

So, would you play? Your answer can depend on your objective, time horizon and exposure. A high expected balance alone does not settle it.
