Student research essay

Davide Di Conno

How can the ergodic theory be used to maximise one's wealth growth? Student research essay; undated.

A public reading link is not yet available.

How it relates to ErgoLabs

Page 4 introduces the coin-toss game. Pages 5–7 discuss losses that prevent further play and ways to limit the money at risk.

The website distinguishes growth rates from averages of wealth, and gradual losses from losing everything. Whether insurance helps depends on the premium and the outcome being assessed. Reducing risk alone does not make wealth ergodic.

Research paper · Compound growth

Ole Peters (2011)

The time resolution of the St Petersburg paradox. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A, 369, 4913–4931.

Read the paper ↗

How it relates to ErgoLabs

Section 6, especially equations (6.6)–(6.10), uses logarithms to calculate compound growth. Section 7 explains how this calculation differs in meaning from treating the logarithm of wealth as a measure of personal value, or utility.

In Peters’ lottery, the ticket price and the player’s wealth affect the fraction at risk. Experiment A instead uses fixed percentage gains and losses. Repeating opportunities on equivalent terms is a model assumption.

Model assumptions, reproducibility & limitations

Each player starts with $100. Independent fair tosses multiply all current wealth by 1.5 or 0.6. The main game has no costs, income, spending, borrowing, pooling or early stopping. Balances may include fractions of a cent.

At round 100, H follows Binomial(100, ½). Wealth is $100 × 1.5ᴴ × 0.6¹⁰⁰⁻ᴴ. Finishing below $100 means H ≤ 55, with exact probability approximately 86.44%.

The simulation uses Mulberry32 to generate its tosses. Reusing a seed reproduces this website’s results; it need not reproduce results from NumPy. The final-wealth calculation has been checked against the number of heads.

The simulation illustrates the model; it does not prove ergodicity. A gap between the mean and median is not enough to establish non-ergodicity either.

Maximising long-run compound growth is one possible aim. A player’s preferences, available money and intended number of rounds can lead to a different choice. This example does not disprove expected-utility theory.
